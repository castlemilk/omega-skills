# Possibility and scenario method

## 1. Start with outcomes and boundaries

Write the question at the user's requested level. For example, "Which physical activities become economical when reasoning is cheap?" is different from "Which feature should a particular instrument have?" List what is inside the system, what is external, and which assumptions may change over the chosen horizon. Founder fit is a later filter unless the brief explicitly requests it first.

## 2. Classify constraints

| Constraint class | Required treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Fundamental physical bound | State the applicable law, domain, units and assumptions; cite a reliable technical source. A bound does not establish achievable engineering performance. |
| Scientific uncertainty | State what is unproven or unmeasured. Keep conditional branches without claiming a breakthrough exists or cannot occur. |
| Engineering limitation | Identify reliability, heat, yield, precision, calibration, packaging, integration or maintenance limits. Ask which are reducible and at what system cost. |
| Resource or economic threshold | Include capital, energy, materials, logistics, labor, support and financing. Separate scarcity in nature from scarcity of usable capacity in a location or period. |
| Institutional or behavioral constraint | Identify permissions, liability, procurement, adoption incentives and trust. These may change; they are not laws of physics. |

Avoid "AI cannot do X" as a timeless axiom. Specify the actual missing observation, actuator, resource, experiment, interface or authority. Inference can sometimes substitute for sensing, but do not claim a variable is uniquely identified from insufficient observations without explicit assumptions. Simulation is useful only within its validation scope; it may either increase or reduce physical testing needs.

## 3. Find threshold shifts

Ask how a change alters not just cost but scale, frequency, acceptable error, latency, autonomy, minimum batch size, or operating environment. Examples are hypothetical mechanisms, not forecasts: a cost reduction could make continuous monitoring affordable; improved reliability could permit longer unattended operation; lower setup cost could make smaller production runs viable.

For each threshold record:

| Field | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Baseline | What is measured today, in which environment and at what total cost? |
| Required change | What value or range must be crossed, and why? |
| Complements | Which other advances, assets, permissions and demand conditions are required? |
| Interaction | Are these substitutes, independent requirements, or coupled bottlenecks? |
| Unlock | Which previously impractical outcome becomes feasible? |
| New limit | What becomes the binding constraint after the change? |
| Test | Which observation distinguishes this mechanism from its strongest alternative? |

Do not multiply independent success probabilities when dependencies are unknown. Use an explicit dependency map and sensitivity ranges. Label toy calculations and check dimensional consistency.

## 4. Search combinations and missing functions

Cross capability changes with enduring needs and operating contexts. Consider measuring, moving, transforming, experimenting, producing, preserving, coordinating and verifying. Vary environments, users, frequency and scale. Include outcomes outside the current portfolio and outcomes that do not yet have conventional product categories.

Keep two registers: **possibility space** (prerequisites and physical plausibility) and **commercial candidates** (payer, alternatives, delivery and capture). An idea can belong only in the first. Do not prematurely discard it or silently promote it to the second.

For each promising combination, build forward from capabilities and backward from the desired outcome. Reconcile the two maps. Look for missing complements, sequencing traps, hidden human labor and resources that several proposed industries would compete to use.

## 5. Extrapolate mechanisms, not just curves

Use reference classes, historical adoption patterns and relevant analogies as checks, not proofs that history must repeat. State important differences. Compare exponential, saturating and step-change paths when justified; identify physical limits, cost floors, diminishing returns and data-range limits. Avoid extrapolating a short trend indefinitely.

Separate capability arrival, deployability, adoption, aggregate output and economic capture. Adoption can lag technical readiness. Better utilization or longer device life can offset unit growth. Lower prices may expand use, but a rebound effect needs a demand mechanism rather than an assumption.

Map reinforcing and balancing loops: cheaper service may increase use; greater use may strain inputs; prices or investment may then change. Track distribution of income and financing alongside total productivity. Do not assume demographic contraction everywhere, inevitable financial collapse, or uniformly expanding purchasing power.

## 6. Compare coherent scenarios

Select two or three consequential uncertainties; do not create an arbitrary grid of every imaginable future. Include a continuation/reference path and a serious counter-thesis. Explain internal consistency, dependencies, transition costs and lead indicators. A scenario is a conditional exploration, not a prediction by itself.

The abundant-intelligence stress set should normally include:

- Cheap cognition, but physical reliability and deployment remain costly.
- Cheap cognition plus cheap design, manufacturing and deployment: more outcomes may coexist with margin compression.
- Capability growth with constrained or concentrated purchasing power: need and ability to pay diverge.

These paths can overlap. Add concentrated AI access, slow progress, policy changes or resource shocks when material. Do not infer equal probabilities from equal space in the report.

## 7. Test defensibility and capture

Distinguish a barrier to entry, an operating burden, temporary scarcity and a repeatable relative advantage. Physical complexity alone is not evidence of pricing power.

For an integrated system, ask what compounds: transferable reliability evidence, production yield, calibration, service reach, authorized access, distribution, or control of a valuable complement. Check ownership, replicability, customer portability, alternative architectures and lifecycle costs. Permissions and standards may favor incumbents or reduce margins as well as create barriers.

Compare who captures the benefit under competition: consumers, workers, customers, asset owners, suppliers or the operator. Include installation, maintenance, exceptions, replacement-cost labor, liability and capital costs in economic estimates. Separate one-time engineering from recurring operating cost.

Finish with robust actions, contingent options and explicit reasons to wait. Prefer the smallest observation that can separate competing mechanisms over a large speculative roadmap.
